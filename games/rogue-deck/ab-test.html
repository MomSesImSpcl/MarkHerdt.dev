Rogue Deck: A/B Test Plan

My playtest logs showed that players almost never buy Common Rogue effects. This page plans the A/B test I would run once Rogue Deck is live: hypothesis, metrics, sample size, analysis and the rules for deciding whether to ship.

The baseline comes from my real playtest logs. Rogue Deck isn't live yet, so the experiment result further down is simulated, using assumed traffic and effect sizes. The analysis code produces every number on this page and can be run on real logs later without changes.

1. The problem

The Rogue shop offers three effects at a time. With rarity weights of 8/4/2/1, more than half of all offers are Common. In 20 logged playtest matches, players made 266 Rogue purchases, and only 6 of them were Common.

The balancing model says Commons are not bad deals. Here's an example offer, with values from the model at a mid-match score of about 100 points:

OfferRarityCostExpected gainNetPayback

Score Multiplier: Hearts ×5Legendary40+68+281.7×

Expected points gained over the rest of the match, from the balancing spreadsheet.

The Common earns back its cost as well as the Rare and better than the Legendary. But it adds fewer points per Rogue slot, and slots are limited. On the other hand, its low price keeps your score higher, so the next slot unlocks sooner. Which choice wins over a whole match isn't obvious from the model alone. That's why this is tested with players, and why final score is a guardrail.

2. Hypothesis & variant

Hypothesis

Players judge Rogue offers by rarity, not by value. If the shop shows what each offer is expected to earn, more players will pick Commons when they are the better deal, without hurting match scores, match length or retention.

  • One change only. The variant changes the shop UI, not prices or rarity weights, so any effect comes from information alone. A Common price change is a separate follow-up test.
  • Both numbers are shown. Showing only the payback ratio would push players towards Commons even when a Rare adds more points. Showing gain and cost lets players judge for themselves.

Guardrails use one-sided non-inferiority checks: the 90% confidence interval has to stay inside the margin. A guardrail passes when the data rules out a meaningful harm, not just when the change isn't significant.

The guardrails set the test size, not the primary metric. Common share needs only a few hundred players, but D7 retention needs thousands. That's why the whole player base is in the test instead of a small slice, and why smaller retention effects are checked in a holdback group after rollout.

6. Analysis plan

Written down before the test starts, so the result can't shape the analysis:

  • No peeking. The test runs the full 14 days, even if the primary metric looks clear after day one. It only stops early for a bug or a severe guardrail breach.
  • Sample ratio check first. A chi-square test on group sizes. If p < 0.001, assignment is broken and nothing else gets analysed until it's fixed.
  • Clustered confidence interval. Common share is a ratio of purchases, but players are the unit of randomization. The confidence interval uses the delta method with players as clusters, cross-checked with a bootstrap that resamples whole players.
  • Novelty check. Week 1 vs week 2. Rollout planning uses the week 2 effect.
  • Segments are exploratory. New vs existing players, 2- vs 4-player matches and game mode are looked at to understand the result, never to rescue a failed test.

What would change the decision

  • Primary CI includes 0: information alone doesn't change behaviour. Next, test a lower Common price instead.
  • Final score falls by more than 5%: the variant steers players into worse buys. Show net points only, or rebalance Commons before showing value.
  • Match length rises by more than 5%: the shop is too slow to read. Simplify to one number per offer and retest.

Follow-up test

If Commons are still rarely bought after rollout, the next test changes the price: for example Common Rogue effects at 5% instead of 10% of your score, with the same metrics and guardrails.
