Rogue Deck: A/B Test Plan

My playtest logs showed that players almost never buy Common Rogue effects. This page plans the A/B test I would run once Rogue Deck is live: hypothesis, metrics, sample size, analysis and the rules for deciding whether to ship.

The baseline comes from my real playtest logs. Rogue Deck isn't live yet, so traffic numbers are assumptions and the test hasn't been run. The analysis code is ready to run on real logs once it is.
Baseline1.4% of Common offers get bought on turns with a purchase (6 of ~426)
VariantShop shows each offer's expected gain and payback
Split50/50, randomized by player
Duration14 days, two full weeks
Ship ifCommons clearly up, all guardrails pass

1. The problem

The Rogue shop always shows three effects to choose from. Commons are the most common rarity (weights 8/4/2/1), so more than half of all offers are Common. But in 20 logged playtest matches, players bought 266 Rogue effects, and only 6 of them were Common.

According to the balancing model, Commons aren't bad buys. Here's an example offer, with values from the model at a mid-match score of about 100 points:

OfferRarityCostExpected gainNet gainPayback
Score Multiplier: Hearts ×2Common10+27+172.7×
Score Multiplier: Red ×2Rare20+54+342.7×
Score Multiplier: Hearts ×5Legendary40+68+281.7×

Expected gain: extra points the effect approximately earns over the rest of the match. Net gain: gain minus cost. Payback: how many times the effect earns back its cost. All values from the balancing spreadsheet.

For its price, the Common is as good as the Rare and better than the Legendary. But you can only own a limited number of Rogue effects, and per effect the Common adds the fewest points. On the other hand, it's cheap, so your score stays higher and you unlock the next Rogue slot sooner.

Which choice wins over a whole match can't be answered by the model alone. That's why it's tested with real players, and why final score is one of the guardrails.

2. Hypothesis & variant

Hypothesis

Players pick Rogue offers by rarity, not by what they're worth. If the shop shows how many points each offer is expected to earn, players will pick Commons more often when they're the better deal. Final scores should stay the same or go up, and retention should stay the same.

  • Only one thing changes. Prices and rarity odds stay the same; the shop only shows more information. So any difference comes from the information alone. Lowering the Common price would be a separate test later.
  • Points and payback are both shown. Payback alone would make Commons look best even when a Rare earns more points. Showing both lets players decide for themselves.

3. Test setup

DecisionChoiceWhy
How players are splitBy account, 50/50A player always sees the same shop, on every device. Splitting by match would show the same player both versions and mix up the results.
ModesNormal, Random and DraftRanked keeps the current shop until the test is decided, so no ranked result depends on which group a player is in.
Mixed matchesAllowedPlayers from both groups can meet in the same match. Common Rogue effects only raise the buyer's own score, so the new shop doesn't change anything for their opponents.
Before the testOne week of A/A testingBoth groups get the current shop. This checks that players are split correctly and everything is logged.

4. Metrics

TypeMetricPasses if…
PrimaryCommon purchases per Common offer, on turns where the player buys a Rogue effectPlayers in B buy clearly more of the Commons they're offered than players in A, not just by chance
GuardrailFinal scoreScores in B are not clearly lower than in A (players must not be pushed into worse buys)
GuardrailD7 retention of new playersNo clear drop during the test.
SecondaryCommon share of purchases, points spent on Rogue effects, purchases per match, time in the shop, win rate of B players when matched against A playersOnly used to explain the result, not to decide

Primary is what the test tries to improve. Guardrails are things that must not get worse.

Only turns with a purchase count. On most turns players buy nothing, because they're saving points or none of the offers fits. Those turns say nothing about Commons, so counting them would hide the effect. Players bought a Rogue effect on 266 turns across 60 player-matches, about 4.4 per match.

Baseline: 266 turns with purchases × 3 offers × 53.3% Common (rarity weights 8/4/2/1) ≈ 426 Common offers. Players bought 6 of them, about 1.4%.

5. Sample size & duration

Goal
Players buy more of the Commons they're offered, and final scores stay the same or go up
Final score dropping, while common purchases rise, would mean that the players are making worse decisions
Players
Enough different players, not just enough offers
One player sees many offers and tends to decide the same way each time. So 1,000 offers from 20 players say less than 1,000 offers from 500 players.
Duration
14 days
Covers two full weeks of weekdays and weekends, and gives the first excitement about the new shop time to wear off.

6. Reading the result

  1. Group sizes. Both groups are about the same size. Otherwise the split is broken and the test is rerun.
  2. Primary metric. Players in B buy clearly more of the Commons they're offered than players in A. The rise also has to be big enough to be worth shipping.
  3. Guardrails. Final scores and D7 retention must not clearly drop.

Follow-up test

If players still rarely buy Commons after the release, the next test changes the price instead, for example 5% of your score instead of 10%. It uses the same metrics and guardrails.
