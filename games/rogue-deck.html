Rogue Deck

A competitive multiplayer card game: as simple as UNO, with Yu-Gi-Oh!-style deck building and roguelike elements. Currently in development.

RoleSolo developer: design, programming
EngineUnity, C#
StatusIn development
Players2–4, competitive

Concept

A card game that is as easy to learn as UNO, with the deck-building depth of Yu-Gi-Oh! and the run-to-run variety of a roguelike. Matches support 2–4 players, in PvE and PvP.

Core loop

Each player has their own standard deck of playing cards (clubs, hearts, spades and diamonds). At the start of a match, a random card is placed in the center. The player can play a card on their turn if it has the same suit as the center card, or its rank is one higher or one lower.

Only the second option gives the player points: a card that is one rank above or below the center card earns its rank as points (Hearts 7 = 7 points), plus any active multipliers. A card that only matches the suit gives no points, but its effect still triggers. So every turn you choose between scoring now and using an effect or getting rid of a card.

The match ends when someone plays their last card, but the winner is the player with the most points, not the first one to empty their hand.

That creates the central tension of every match: do I keep playing cards to build my score, or race to empty my hand and end the match while I'm ahead?

Cards & the Rogue Deck

Card effects

Every card has an effect that triggers when it's played, for example:

  • Opponents draw X cards
  • Opponents reveal their hand for X turns
  • This card gets a point multiplier

Choosing when to play which card is a scoring decision as well as a tactical one.

The Rogue Deck

Alongside their normal deck, each player has a Rogue Deck: effect cards bought with points that stay active for the rest of the match and apply to all of that player's cards. Examples:

  • Score multipliers for all cards, one color, or one suit
  • Play 1 additional card every turn
  • Every card you play gives you points, even suit-only matches

Players can activate one Rogue card per turn, up to 10 in total (each slot unlocks at a score threshold), and the Rogue Deck is reshuffled every turn. Because Rogue cards cost points, every purchase is a trade-off: get stronger for later, or protect your lead now.

Readability as a design rule

I deliberately kept effects short, mostly one sentence, so every player understands a card at first glance. The goal behind this was to prevent the problem modern YuGiOh has: It can take minutes to understand an effect ; Rogue Deck gets its depth from how simple cards combine, not from complicated text.

Rarity & probability design

Both decks use four rarities. Rarity controls how often powerful effects appear, which is the main lever for keeping matches varied without letting the strongest effects show up every game.

Effect catalog

The deck uses 18 effects in 35 rarity variants. Many effects exist at several rarities, so higher rarities scale the same idea instead of adding new rules. This keeps every card readable while still giving rare cards a clear power jump.

Together, the two rules shape the key decisions:

  • Every purchase hurts the same: because the price scales with your score, a Legendary always costs 40% of what you have, whether you buy it on turn 3 or turn 15. Leaders pay more in absolute points, which keeps matches close.
  • Rarity is a real trade-off: a stronger effect takes a bigger bite out of your score, so a Common that fits your hand can be the better buy than a Legendary that doesn't.
  • The thresholds pace the match: the first slots open almost immediately, while slot 6 needs 500 points, more than an average winning score. Late slots are only reachable in exceptional runs.

Multiplier stacking

All multipliers add up instead of multiplying each other. With a Spades ×2 and an all-cards ×3, spades score ×5 and every other card ×3. Example: a Clubs 7 played with three Legendary Rogue effects and a ×5 card effect:

Additive stacking was a deliberate balancing choice. If the same multipliers were multiplied, that card would score 7 × 3 × 4 × 5 × 5 = 2,100 points instead of 119. Power grows steadily with each upgrade instead of exploding, so a player who gets lucky early can still be caught.

Power scaling by rarity

Scaled effects grow in steady steps from Common to Legendary. The step size differs per effect type: card draw and hidden turns grow by +1 per rarity, while multipliers grow from ×2 to ×5, because points are the win condition and need the strongest rarity gap.

Balancing

Balancing has mainly been done through playtesting: playing many matches, spotting cards that were too strong or never mattered, and adjusting their values. The balancing spreadsheet collects every effect, its rarity and value, and the rarity math in one place.

The strongest pairs all combine mass card plays (Additional Card Plays, Play All…) with mass scoring (All Cards Give Points). The win condition keeps them in check: the match is won by points, not by emptying your hand. Even a player who discards their entire hand in one turn still needs the highest score to win.

Most of these effects also sit at Epic and Legendary, so their low drop rates make it unlikely that a player holds a full combo. It is still possible to get these combinations, and that's on purpose. In playtests, the rare runs where I pulled several of the strongest cards were some of the most fun matches: a once-in-a-while power spike is part of the roguelike appeal.

What the data shows

  • The Rogue system drives the score. Without multipliers, an average turn is worth about 5 points. Winners earn 24–34 points per turn, so roughly 80% of their points come from multipliers and effects, exactly the role the Rogue Deck was designed for.
  • Getting stronger has a real price. Winners spent about a third of everything they earned on Rogue effects. Purchases happen early, around turn 7, at a score of about 100, so each one costs around 30 points: a noticeable dent, but one that pays off over the rest of the match.
  • The unlock thresholds set the ceiling. No 2-player match got past slot 5, and slot 6 (500 points) was only reached in the highest-scoring 4-player matches. Late slots stay a rare jackpot instead of a goal.
  • Every effect is worth buying, but players chase rarity. Priced at the score players actually had when buying, every Rogue effect earns back its cost over the rest of the match, from about 1.7× for Legendary single-suit multipliers up to 7–10× for Additional Card Plays. Commons pay back as well as most higher rarities, yet only 6 of 266 purchases were Common, although Commons make up half of all offers. That is the next thing to address: making cheap Commons feel as attractive as their numbers say they are.

Design iterations

Most of the rules above didn't start that way. These are the changes that shaped the current balance, and one idea I tested and dropped.

1. Rogue cards: from fixed prices to a share of your score

Before
Every Rogue slot had a fixed price. The prices rose from slot to slot, but were lower than today's unlock thresholds.
Problem
The further a match went, the more extreme the fixed prices became. Buying a new card was often no longer worth it, so the Rogue system stalled in the late game.
Change
The price became a share of your current score, set by rarity (10–40%). The old fixed prices became the unlock thresholds for each slot.
Result
Every purchase hurts the same at any point in the match, and leaders pay more in absolute points. The playtest logs confirm it: every Rogue effect now pays back its cost.

2. Multipliers: from multiplicative to additive

Before
Multipliers multiplied each other.
Problem
A few lucky draws could make one run explode, which made matches very unbalanced. The scores also became huge numbers that were hard to read and added nothing to the game.
Change
All multipliers now add up instead (see Multiplier stacking).
Result
The same Clubs 7 example scores 119 instead of 2,100. Power grows steadily, a player who gets lucky early can still be caught, and scores stay readable.

3. One Rogue activation per turn

Problem
A player with a lot of points could buy several strong cards in a single turn, for example three Legendaries at once, and run away with the match.
Change
Only one Rogue card can be activated per turn.
Result
Even with plenty of points and several great offers, players have to choose. A big lead turns into one decision per turn, not an instant power spike.

4. Ten Rogue slots per match

Problem
A low cap would end every run at roughly the same power level, so exceptional runs would have nothing left to aim for.
Change
10 slots, each unlocked by a score threshold that rises steeply (10 up to 5,000 points).
Result
The game stays open-ended: in playtests, no match got past slot 6, so the late slots remain something great runs can strive for, not the average outcome.

Tested and dropped: same-rank cards score points

Idea
Let cards of the same rank as the center card score, not just ranks one higher or lower.
Problem
Card plays per round went up. Only slightly, but noticeably. It also would have made the Rare effect Play All Cards With Same Rank far stronger, because it would score with every card.
Decision
Kept scoring at rank ±1 only. Same-rank plays stay a strategic choice, and the effect has to be used at the right moment.

Next experiment: making Commons attractive

Common Rogue effects are underbought

Evidence
Commons make up half of all offers and pay back their cost as well as most higher rarities, yet only 6 of 266 logged purchases were Common.
Hypothesis
Players judge offers by rarity, not by value. Commons look weak, even though their low price makes them good deals.
Test
Try one change at a time, e.g. a lower Common price or showing each offer's expected payback in the shop, then replay the same 2- and 4-player match set with the event logger.
Measure
Primary: Common share of purchases. Guardrails: winning score and points spent on Rogue effects should stay close to today's values.

Challenges & solutions

Keeping combos exciting without letting them break the game. The fun of a roguelike comes from powerful synergies, but in a competitive match one dominant combination ruins it for everyone else. The tools against this are Rogue cards that cost points (so getting strong means falling behind first), the limit of one activation per turn and 10 per match, and reshuffling the Rogue Deck every turn so no player can rely on a fixed combo.
